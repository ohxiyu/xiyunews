Solana's new consensus protocol Alpenglow goes live on mainnet

Background

Background

TowerBFT had been Solana's consensus mechanism since the network launched in March 2020. Alpenglow's design was published in May 2025 by Anza's research team led by ETH Zurich professor Roger Wattenhofer with Quentin Kniep and Kobi Sliwinski. Before mainnet, the protocol went through public testing including red teaming, penetration testing, repeated migration rehearsals and a security competition, followed by testnet and devnet launches. Further upgrades — Rotor for block data propagation, geographically aware leader rotation, and a post-quantum research design called Quantumglow — are planned but have no committed dates.

Anza announced that Alpenglow, Solana's new consensus protocol, is now live on mainnet-beta, retiring TowerBFT. The switchover took place at genesis slot 459,653,000, activated at epoch 1064, with 654 validators participating and a handoff that took 900 milliseconds, cutting transaction finality from about 12.8 seconds to roughly 150 milliseconds.

The change targets finality — the point at which a transaction can no longer be reversed and the moment an exchange, bank or merchant waits for before treating an asset as moved. Anza CEO Brennan Watt called it "the largest, most ambitious blockchain upgrade since 'The Merge,'" saying the team took it from white paper to mainnet in under 18 months.

Validators approved the upgrade in September 2025, voting 98% in favor of SIMD-0236, the implementing proposal; Anza shipped the protocol in Agave, its validator client, and independent operators coordinated a shared handoff point on the running network while carrying the existing ledger forward. The design is intended to keep working with less than 20% of stake acting maliciously and a further 20% offline, gradually lengthening block times if the network goes 10 seconds without finalizing a block.

Analysis and references

Impact analysis

The upgrade directly affects Solana's base-layer settlement speed, the channel through which exchanges, custodians and payment processors decide when a SOL or token transfer counts as settled; shorter finality could change deposit-crediting and withdrawal policies on centralized venues and reduce the confirmation wait baked into on-chain trading and bridging flows. It also alters validator vote mechanics, since votes are now sent directly between validators and weighted by stake rather than written into later blocks, which bears on validator operations and staking economics.

OCC Finalizes First GENIUS Act Rule: No Safe Harbor for Existing Stablecoin Rewa

Background

Background

The GENIUS Act is the federal statute establishing a framework for payment stablecoins, and the OCC is the primary banking regulator tasked with implementing it for issuers under its oversight. The rule applies to national banks, federal savings associations, federal branches and their subsidiaries, nonbank firms that seek or receive federal qualified issuer status, foreign payment stablecoin issuers, and state-qualified issuers where the OCC has regulatory or enforcement authority. Comptroller of the Currency Jonathan Gould described the framework as providing "strong standards for reserve backing, redemption, and risk management." Anti-money-laundering, Bank Secrecy Act and sanctions requirements are being handled in a separate rulemaking with Treasury.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency finalized its first rule implementing the GENIUS Act on Wednesday, covering payment stablecoin issuance, custody and third-party partnerships for entities under its jurisdiction, with the rule and the statute taking effect January 18, 2027. Existing stablecoin yield and rewards programs receive no automatic grandfathering or safe harbor and will be assessed for compliance case by case.

Because reward and yield programs are a material revenue line for major exchanges and issuers such as Coinbase, PayPal and Kraken, the refusal to grandfather existing programs means those products face individual compliance review rather than continuing automatically once the framework takes effect.

The final rule requires issuers to hold reserves worth at least as much as their outstanding stablecoins and generally to process redemption requests within two business days. The OCC dropped a proposed presumption that certain arrangements involving affiliates or related third parties violate the yield ban, while retaining the prohibition on issuers paying interest or yield solely for holding, using or retaining stablecoins, and said it will announce separately when it begins processing issuer applications and waiver requests.

Impact analysis

Reserve, redemption and custody standards also set compliance costs for entities seeking OCC-supervised issuer status.

Eight Bits of Trust: eMudhra's CA keys cracked in milliseconds

Background

Background

eMudhra is an Indian certificate authority founded in 2008; per Wikipedia it has issued more than 60 million digital certificates. Normally a CA key compromise involves theft, with a clear moment of loss and a question of who holds the key. Here nothing was stolen — the key material was so weak it could simply be computed. Fixing the updatable devices is largely a matter of refreshing CA bundles (OS ca-certificates, certifi, Node, Homebrew, container base images), while long-lived embedded devices are the hard part.

The private keys behind eight of eMudhra's intermediate CA certificates were found to be integers smaller than 256 — effectively eight bits of entropy — and all eight were recovered in about 11 milliseconds. The finding was disclosed on October 20 at emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com, and major browsers and software platforms have begun blocking the affected certificates.

Because the weak keys were effectively public from the day the certificates were issued, anyone could have forged certificates chaining to a trusted root — and devices that cannot update their CA bundles remain exposed.

According to the original reporter, the discovery came out of Google's open-source paranoid_crypto tool, run essentially out of the box against the CCADB corpus before subscriber certificates were examined. A follow-up sweep of all existing CCADB certificates reportedly turned up no further hits, and Chrome found no rogue certificates in Certificate Transparency logs.

Analysis and references

Impact analysis

The transmission path runs through web PKI trust rather than on-chain assets: forged certificates could support phishing or interception against crypto exchange front-ends, wallet download pages and firmware update channels that rely on public TLS. Hardware and IoT wallets whose trust stores cannot be refreshed carry the longest-lived exposure, though no market segment is directly impaired by the key weakness itself.

Discussion

Hacker News commenters focused on the sheer number of trusted CAs — one remark that "having 100 trusted certificate authorities might have been excessive" — and on the unpatched-device problem, since the advice reduces to "update them" for hardware that often cannot be updated. One commenter floated the idea of using the broken keys to mint certificates for jailbreaking locked-down IoT devices, while another linked a prior, heavily discussed HN thread on the eMudhra intermediate CA compromise.

Polkadot Community Foundation: Fearless Wallet browser extension contains code that leaks private keys, users must move assets immediately

Background

Background

Fearless Wallet is a Polkadot- and Kusama-focused wallet offered as both a browser extension and a mobile app; the Foundation stressed that it is developed and maintained by an independent team and is not an official Polkadot wallet. PAPI Rescue describes itself as a technical recovery service for compromised wallets on Polkadot-based chains, adapting its product to each eligible case. On Polkadot, staked DOT is bonded and locked, which complicates moving funds out of a compromised account.

The Polkadot Community Foundation posted a security warning on the Polkadot forum stating that versions 3.0.1, 3.0.3, 3.0.4 and 3.0.5 of Fearless Wallet's browser extension contain malicious code that sends users' private keys to attacker-controlled servers when the wallet is unlocked. Eight users have so far reported unauthorized transactions attributable to the incident, totaling 17,286 DOT.

The Chrome version is confirmed affected, and a delisted Firefox version used the same code; no such code was found in the Android or iOS mobile apps, though any private key previously imported into the browser extension should still be considered leaked. Users whose assets are staked, in nomination pools, or locked in governance votes can first seek help through PAPI Rescue, a tool developed by the polkadot-api team.

Background

Brockman said the bug sat in PHP for 14 years, with a limited version surfacing in 2024 that "wasn't considered significant at the time," and the release was moved up from an original October 22 target to October 21 after details began leaking. OpenAI said it is also fixing "several other significant bugs," including a Node.js remote code execution flaw announced Wednesday, and has committed $1 billion in subsidized access to Daybreak, its program for defenders of critical infrastructure.

LiteLLM Poisoned for the Second Time This Year: Steals Credentials Without Even Being Invoked

Background

Background

LiteLLM is an open-source AI gateway (LLM proxy) that puts an AI stack behind one OpenAI-compatible API, routing requests to models such as OpenAI and Anthropic and centrally managing API keys, with self-hosting including air-gapped deployments. This is the second supply chain attack on the project this year, after an earlier incident in March.

Security firm JFrog found that LiteLLM version 1.107.0 and its official Docker images were compromised in a supply chain attack, with three extra files hidden in the Python package. Once the Python environment starts, the malicious code runs even if LiteLLM is never called, harvesting browser passwords, SSH private keys, AWS and other cloud credentials, and authentication data for tools such as Claude, Codex and Cursor, then sending it to a server disguised as an official domain.

JFrog advises anyone who installed or ran 1.107.0 to treat the related credentials as potentially leaked, isolate and rebuild the environment, and rotate API keys rather than simply uninstalling the software; version 1.106.2 is the confirmed safe rollback. The attacker also left a persistent backdoor that checks for remote instructions once an hour and can download and execute new code.

PyPI has removed the malicious version and Docker Hub has taken down the affected images. The official source code on GitHub does not contain the malicious files, yet all seven platform packages published to PyPI were implanted with the trojan; Docker build records show the release system pulled source from a look-alike repository whose name differs from the official one by a single hyphen, and how it entered the release pipeline remains unclear.
