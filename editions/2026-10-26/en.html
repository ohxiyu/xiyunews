Initia hack drove its USDC bridge reserve down to $63 as withdrawals failed

Background

Background

Initia is a Layer 1 blockchain built with the Cosmos SDK that aims to link a main network with a set of interwoven application rollups. Its native bridge is designed to move assets between Initia, its rollups and other blockchains, which made the bridge reserve a single point of dependency for users moving USDC. Bridge exploits have been a recurring attack vector across crypto, with on-chain trackers such as DefiLlama maintaining databases of such incidents.

Initia suffered a bridge exploit on October 23 that drained its USDC bridge reserve down to $63, with users unable to complete withdrawals. Reporting on the incident states roughly $3 million in real assets exited through bridges against $7 million in unbacked balances.

The reserve depletion left the bridge unable to honor user withdrawals, turning a protocol security breach into a direct loss of access for holders who relied on the bridge to move USDC on and off Initia.

The reported gap between the ~$3 million in real assets that left through bridges and the $7 million in unbacked balances indicates the shortfall exceeds the value actually withdrawn. The incident reduced the USDC bridge reserve to $63.

Impact analysis

The incident concentrates on Initia's bridge and stablecoin liquidity: users seeking to exit via USDC on that route face a reserve that can no longer cover redemptions, and the $7 million in unbacked balances reported implies a shortfall to be resolved rather than a simple liquidity crunch. Background

Background

Redeemable debt carries a fixed maturity date on which the issuer must repay the principal to holders. GPU-backed lending and project-level debt for AI data centers have grown into a distinct financing channel as AI compute build-outs scale, with lenders focusing on hardware commitments, delivery timing and counterparty credit quality. An investment-grade rating generally broadens access to such credit by signaling a relatively lower assessed default risk.

Elon Musk said SpaceX has completed $40 billion of redeemable GPU debt financing, with the proceeds mainly earmarked for GPUs scheduled to arrive in the first and second quarters. According to BlockBeats, Musk credited the company's investment-grade credit rating and revenue from its Starlink satellite-internet and launch businesses with keeping its access to credit open.

As described, the package would be among the largest disclosed debt raises aimed specifically at buying AI compute, indicating that GPU purchases are being funded through credit markets rather than only from operating cash flow. Musk said the spending will have little impact on the current quarter because the hardware has not yet arrived.

Musk described the instrument as redeemable debt — borrowing with a defined repayment date — but the flash report did not disclose lenders, coupon, maturity or other terms, and there was no independent confirmation of the financing. BlockBeats listed the borrower under the ticker SPCX.O, although SpaceX is not a publicly listed company.

AI agents help rescue ETH’s Glamsterdam fork, ‘Steal My Bitcoin’ site: Hodler’s Digest

Background

Background

Glamsterdam is Ethereum's next scheduled hard fork after Pectra and Fusaka, with published estimates pointing to mainnet in 2026 and its confirmed headliners being EIP-7732 (enshrined proposer-builder separation) and EIP-7928 (block-level access lists). EIP-8037 and EIP-8038 would raise the cost of creating and accessing state so that fees better reflect resource consumption under a proposed throughput scaling plan — the changes at the centre of the cancelled test.

Ethereum's second and final Glamsterdam hard fork testnet on Hoodi was cancelled this week after ChainSecurity found vulnerabilities in the gas-scaling EIPs 8037 and 8038 that could break at least 13 DeFi contracts when a call runs out of gas. Researchers at Eth Labs and the Ethereum Foundation independently spun up AI agents to scan contracts for the flaws, then set up a shared message board in the form of a GitHub repo to coordinate the agents' work.

The cancellation postpones the dress rehearsal for an upgrade that Ethereum DeFi operators depend on, and Eth Labs' Derek Chiang said protocols designers should in future do "much more thorough analysis of potential breakages" much earlier in the process. He added that the agent exercise proved effective enough that a second board has been opened to study EIP-8198, or "Quick Slots", proposed for the follow-up Hegota fork.

Chiang said the vulnerability was found "a few minutes to midnight," causing everyone to scramble, and the Hoodi testnet is not expected to be rescheduled until all of the fork's second-order effects have been properly analyzed. He likened the setup to the OpenAI–Hugging Face hack, where rogue agents coordinated through a message board, noting "the same pattern is useful for the good agents too."

Analysis and references

Impact analysis

The transmission runs through the upgrade timeline rather than a live exploit: the flaw surfaced during a Hoodi testnet rehearsal, but DeFi contracts that rely on current gas behavior face review before the fork can be rescheduled. A delay to Glamsterdam also defers the expected effects of enshrined proposer-builder separation and block-level access lists on block building and staking economics.

Cerebras Deploys Trillion-Parameter AI Models

Background

Background

Cerebras builds wafer-scale engines — the WSE-3, and the WSE-3T that powers its CS-4 rack-scale system — and sells access to them through AI training and inference cloud APIs. Serving models of trillion-parameter scale typically involves disaggregated prefill and decode workers that exchange KV-cache data over a network, a pattern the Cerebras post discusses for its own hardware.

Cerebras published a technical blog post explaining how it deploys and serves trillion-parameter frontier models on its wafer-scale systems, covering the pipeline architecture, an interconnect strategy in which only activations are passed between wafers via RDMA over Ethernet, and continuous request streaming instead of GPU-style batching. The post drew a Hacker News thread with 223 points and 69 comments that questioned its benchmarking and prefill performance claims.

The post is a rare public description of serving frontier-scale models on non-GPU wafer-scale hardware, and Hacker News commenters directly disputed parts of the performance case it makes.

Commenters noted that the post cites full-precision benchmarks while the served models run at 4-bit quantization, and that it does not explain how prefill stays fast over the RDMA interconnect. Others asked whether the "unlike a GPU batch" streaming behavior reflects a GPU hardware limit or the absence of equivalent software scaffolding.
